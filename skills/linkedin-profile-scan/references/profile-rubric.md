# Rubric and rewrite patterns

Twelve metrics, three sections. Each metric scores 0–5; that is the unit of judgement and
it stays integer-clean. Section and overall results are then **reported as a grade out of
100** — the share of available points earned — so the number the reader sees is on the
familiar 0–100 scale rather than an odd /60.

| Section | Metrics | Raw max | How it reaches /100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profile | 5 | 25 | raw ÷ 25 × 100 |
| Content | 4 | 20 | raw ÷ 20 × 100 |
| Activity | 3 | 15 | raw ÷ 15 × 100 |
| **Overall** | 12 | 60 | raw ÷ 60 × 100 |

The overall is the weighted average across all twelve checks, so the three section grades
will not add up to it — that is expected, the same way subject grades on a report card do
not sum to the GPA.

Bands, applied to the overall grade and to each section's grade:

| Band | Range | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | 0–39 | Empty, or contradicting itself. A reader cannot tell what this person does. |
| Developing | 40–59 | Filled in, but only with duties and labels. Nothing proves they can do the work. |
| Good | 60–74 | It works. The niche is clear and there are specifics — but no numbers, no proof that closes the question. |
| Strong | 75–89 | Every claim is backed by a result. A recruiter sees what they did and what came of it. |
| Excellent | 90–100 | The profile brings people in by itself. Niche, proof and steady content work together. |

Report the band alongside the number every time. "63" means nothing on its own; "63,
Good" tells the person which of these five paragraphs describes them, and the report
should carry these descriptions where the reader first meets the band name.

Anchors are listed below; scores between them are fine. Say which two anchors the case
sits between and why — that sentence is usually more useful to the person than the number.

The second half of this file, [Rewrite patterns](#rewrite-patterns), is what turns the
score into something actionable. Read it before drafting replacement text.

## Profile — 25

### headline_clarity
- 0 missing, generic, or just a job title
- 1 role only, no context
- 3 clear role and expertise, no differentiation
- 4 clear, specific, differentiated
- 5 also names the audience and the value they get

The 4→5 step is the one almost nobody takes. A headline listing roles and specialisms is a
4. It becomes a 5 when a reader learns who this person helps and what changes for them.

### about_positioning
- 0 missing
- 1 present but generic — adjectives about themselves, no substance
- 3 clear but basic: what they do, no proof
- 4 strong positioning with relevant proof
- 5 positioning, proof, a sense of how they work, and a reason to reply

Check About against the headline. A headline claiming a niche and an About that never
mentions it is worse than either alone — it reads as a profile nobody maintains.

Soft-skill self-description ("detail-oriented", "fast learner", "passionate") is not
positioning. It is unverifiable, everyone writes it, and it costs the space where proof
would go. Its presence usually caps this metric at 2.

### featured_usage
- 0 not used
- 2 used, but the items are weak or stale
- 3 relevant items present
- 4 strong proof, well chosen
- 5 a tight portfolio that visibly backs the headline's claim

Certificates and course completions are evidence of studying, not of capability. They sit
at 2 however many there are. What belongs here: a case study, a shipped product, a talk, a
published article, a piece of work someone can look at.

### visual_profile_quality
- 0 no photo, or unusable
- 2 basic — a photo, default banner
- 3 professional photo, some visual care
- 4 strong and consistent
- 5 polished, distinctive, and the banner carries a message

A custom banner that is merely decorative is a 4. The step to 5 is the banner doing work:
saying what they do, showing the product, naming the audience.

### experience_impact
- 0 roles listed with no descriptions
- 2 mostly duties
- 3 some outcomes
- 4 strong results with evidence
- 5 consistently outcome-shaped, with numbers and scope

Bullets opening with "Responsible for", "Working with", "Creating", "Participating in"
describe duties. Look for what changed, by how much, at what scale.

Judge this across **every role in the profile**, not only the recent ones — the data
collection pulls the full Experience page for exactly this reason. A profile whose recent
roles show outcomes but whose older ones are bare duty-lists is still incomplete, and the
oldest roles often hide the clearest before/after story of how the person grew.

## Content — 20

Window: 90 days. A 30-day window is too tight for a one-off scan — a single quiet month
would misrepresent someone who posts steadily.

### posting_cadence
`0 posts → 0 · 1–2 → 1 · 3–5 → 2 · 6–9 → 3 · 10–15 → 4 · 16+ → 5`

### original_content_share
Share of posts that are their own writing rather than reshares.
`0–19% → 0 · 20–39% → 1 · 40–59% → 2 · 60–74% → 3 · 75–89% → 4 · 90–100% → 5`

With fewer than four posts this swings wildly. Score it, and flag the small base rather
than presenting it as a stable finding.

### content_quality
- 0 no original thought — links, reposts, greetings
- 2 generic professional content that anyone in the field could have written
- 3 useful, specific, worth reading
- 4 real expertise and a point of view
- 5 consistently distinctive, grounded in their own experience or evidence

Event recaps and thank-you posts top out at 3 however well written, because the reader
learns about an event rather than from the author. The step to 4 is teaching something
only this person could teach.

### content_consistency
- 0 no pattern
- 2 sporadic, unrelated topics
- 3 some recurring themes
- 4 consistent rhythm and themes
- 5 clear pillars, reliable rhythm, and a topic people associate with them

Two things are judged: rhythm, and whether the themes match what the person says they
want to be known for. Posting reliably about something other than their stated focus caps
this at 3 — and that gap is worth naming explicitly, because people rarely notice it in
their own feed.

## Activity — 15

Window: 90 days.

### outbound_comments
Substantive comments on **other people's** posts.
`0 → 0 · 1–4 → 1 · 5–14 → 2 · 15–29 → 3 · 30–59 → 4 · 60+ → 5`

"Great post", "thanks", and emoji-only replies do not count. Count them separately and
report how many were dropped — that difference is usually the whole story of this metric,
and it is the cheapest thing on the entire scorecard to fix.

### replies_to_own_comments
Whether they answer people who comment on their posts, and whether the reply continues
the conversation or closes it. Answering most comments is 4; answering in a way that
keeps the thread going is 5. Leaving comments unanswered is 0–1 regardless of how good
the post was — it teaches people that commenting there goes nowhere.

### engagement_relevance
How well the people and content they engage with match what they say they want. This is
about aim, not volume: a handful of comments landing consistently in the right community
scores high. Scattered engagement across unrelated fields scores low even at high volume.

# Rewrite patterns

The score says what is wrong. This section is how to fix it in the person's actual words.
Draft from what is visible in their profile — never invent an achievement.

## Headline

```
[Role] · [Domain or niche] · [What the audience gets]
```

Three options, differing in what they lead with: role-first, niche-first, outcome-first.
Under 220 characters each. Include the niche even when it feels narrowing — a headline
that could describe ten thousand people is why search never surfaces them.

**Before:** `Product Designer at Acme`
**After, three ways:**
- `Product Designer · B2B SaaS · Turning complex admin tools into interfaces people finish tasks in`
- `B2B SaaS designer · Design systems that survive three product teams · 7 years`
- `I make dense enterprise UI usable · Product Designer, design systems, B2B SaaS`

## About

Five moves, roughly 120–200 words:

1. **Hook** — one line stating the problem they solve, not a greeting
2. **What they do, for whom** — the positioning sentence
3. **Proof** — two or three specifics with numbers, scope or names
4. **How they work** — the thing that makes working with them different
5. **A reason to reply** — what to contact them about

Write in first person. Delete every adjective the person could not prove. If a fact is
missing, mark the slot — `[N years]`, `[product name]` — so they can see exactly what to
fill in. Write the marker in the language the rest of the drafted text is in.

## Experience bullets

```
[Verb] [what, at what scale] → [what changed, with a number]
```

**Before:** `Working with developers and engineers to build the platform`
**After:** `Shipped the platform's core interface with a team of [N] engineers — [outcome, e.g. onboarding time down from X to Y]`

Cover every role in the profile — none is skipped. Depth scales with recency and with how
much evidence the role left behind: the recent roles get a full bullet-by-bullet rewrite,
the older ones a targeted line or two that fixes the weakest bullet. What you must not do
is quietly stop after the first two roles, or manufacture a metric for a role a decade old
just to fill the shape. Where the outcome is unknown, keep the scale — scale alone already
beats a bare duty, and an honest "designed the eSports tournament platform" outreads an
invented conversion number.

## Featured

Name three to five specific items drawn from what they actually have: their strongest
post, a case study, a talk, a shipped product, an article. Say what to remove as well as
what to add — a Featured section holding two certificates is worse than an empty one,
because it answers "what can this person do?" with "attend things".

## Content pillars

Propose three, derived from what they already post plus what their headline claims. For
each, one sentence on what it covers and one example post title. Three is the number that
holds: fewer reads as monotony, more reads as no focus at all.

# Report structure

The artifact page follows `assets/report-template.html`. Content, in order:

1. **Score** out of 100 with its band, the three section grades (each also out of 100),
   and the band legend directly beneath them — the reader meets the word "Developing" at
   the section bars, so that is where it has to be explained
2. **What is working** — two or three things, briefly, with evidence
3. **What is costing them** — ranked by points recoverable per unit of effort. Rewriting
   existing text almost always outranks new activity, because those points are already
   sitting on the page waiting to be claimed
4. **The rewrites** — headline options, About draft, Experience bullets, Featured list
5. **Content pillars and a cadence** they can actually keep
6. **Method note** — which route was used, what was not visible, what is therefore `null`

Keep the ranking honest about effort. "Post four times a month" and "rewrite your About
this evening" are not comparable asks, and a list that presents them as equals gets
abandoned at the first hard item.
